Preface  
 
 
This document was prepared to assist International Space Station, and other program 
hardware customers with the flammability configuration analyses required to justify the 
use of flammable materials in flight hardware.  The document provides guidance in 
conducting the flammability assessments required for International Space Station 
payload hardware by SSP 51700, "Payload Safety Policy and Requirements for the 
International Space Station".  It may also be used to assess flammability hazards in 
flight hardware other than payloads, as described in NASA-STD--6001, Flammability, 
Offgassing, and Compatibility Requirements and Test Procedures and NASA-STD-
6016, Standard Materials and Processes Requirements for Spacecraft.  It explains 
procedures and techniques that are considered by NASA to meet the intent of the safety 
requirements, but it does not preclude alternative approaches.

FLAMMABILITY CONFIGURATION ANALYSIS FOR SPACECRAFT 
APPLICATIONS 
 
1. 
SCOPE 
 
1.1 
Purpose 
 
This handbook provides guidance for flammability configuration analyses required to justify the 
use of flammable materials in spacecraft flight hardware used in crew breathing environments.  It 
does not address materials flammability concerns associated with propulsion systems or with 
high-pressure oxygen systems.  
 
Fire is one of the many potentially catastrophic hazards associated with the operation of crewed 
spacecraft. A major lesson learned by NASA from the Apollo 204 fire in 1966 was that ignition 
sources in an electrically powered vehicle should and can be minimized but can never be 
eliminated. For this reason, spacecraft fire control is based on minimizing potential ignition 
sources and minimizing materials that can propagate fire. Fire extinguishers have always been 
provided on crewed spacecraft to provide a means for the crew to respond to a fire but are not 
considered as part of the fire control process. 
 
“Minimizing materials that can propagate fire” does not mean eliminating all flammable 
materials―the cost of designing and building spacecraft using only nonflammable materials is 
extraordinary and unnecessary. It means controlling the quantity and configuration of such 
materials to eliminate potential fire propagation paths and ensure that any fire would be small, 
localized, and isolated, and would self-extinguish without harm to the crew. 
 
Over the years, NASA has developed many solutions for controlling the configuration of 
flammable materials, including potentially flammable materials in commercial off-the-shelf 
hardware, so that they can be used safely in air and oxygen-enriched environments in crewed 
spacecraft. This handbook describes and explains these design solutions so payload customers 
and other organizations can use them in designing safe and cost-effective flight hardware. 
 
Whenever possible, the testing that forms the basis of these guidelines is referenced. In many 
cases, guidelines are based on historical experience without references. The guidelines without 
references were originally published in 1988 as the Space Shuttle Program (SSP) document, 
NSTS 22648, Flammability Configuration Analysis for Space Applications. These guidelines 
have been used successfully for over 30 years and have been considered to be acceptably 
conservative.   
 
Recent testing has shown that some of the guidelines in previous releases of this handbook were 
not adequate.  The concerns raised by this testing were documented in JSC memo ES4-24-030, a 
copy of which can be found in Appendix B.  In addition, a previous guideline on minimum 
power levels required for ignition of materials has been found to be incorrect and 
unconservative.  This revision attempts to correct such inadequate and incorrect guidance in the

Proper application of this handbook will produce acceptable flammability configurations for

3.2 
Definitions  
 
Barrier, Fire: An obstruction (such as a partition, enclosure, or shielding material) that prohibits 
or tends to inhibit ignition and burning propagation of flammable protected materials. A fire 
barrier material meets ignition resistance and shielding requirements when the material prevents 
ignition of flammable materials it is designed to protect when subjected to NASA-STD-6001, 
Flammability, Offgassing, and Compatibility Requirements and Test Procedures, Test 1. 
 
Break, Fire: A gap, opening, or nonflammable material between flammable materials which 
would prevent propagation of burning. 
 
Containers, Intermediate: Containers that have no active vents or cooling airflow but are not 
physically sealed to prevent air exchange. 
 
Containers, Sealed: Containers that are enclosed adequately enough to preclude the 
replenishment of a combustible atmosphere under conditions of a fire. 
 
Containers, Vented: Containers that have active vents, are unsealed, and permit atmosphere 
exchange. Vented containers may or may not contain forced air convection. 
 
Containment, Fire: The situation in which a fire and/or burning material does not progress 
beyond the confines of a configuration.

Enriched Oxygen (and Oxygen Enriched): Environments containing oxygen concentrations 
higher than the 20.9 percent in terrestrial air. 
 
Flammable: Describes a material that fails to meet acceptance criteria when tested for 
flammability according to the requirements of NASA-STD-6001.  
 
Heat Sink: A structure or panel of high thermal conductivity in contact with a flammable 
material that extracts sufficient heat by conduction to lower the temperature below the ignition 
point and prevent it from propagating fire.  
 
Ignition Source: A source of heat sufficiently intense and localized to induce combustion. 
Potential ignition sources in spacecraft cabin environments are almost entirely electrical, but not 
all electrical sources are potential ignition sources (see section 4.1 in this handbook). Chemical 
ignition sources must be considered.  
 
Nonflammable: Describes a material that meets the acceptance criteria when tested according to 
the requirements of NASA-STD-6001.  
 
Propagation Paths: The paths taken by a flame front that represent fire paths between 
flammable materials. They are not necessarily straight or coplanar.  
 
Responsible Hardware Organization: Organization responsible for designing the hardware and 
certifying it for flight. 
 
Vent:  Opening that allows the cabin atmosphere to enter or pass out of a container.  Active 
vents have forced air convection driven by a fan, whereas passive vents are openings for pressure 
equalization without forced air convection. 
 
Void Space: Unoccupied volume in a container. 
 
 
4. 
TECHNICAL REQUIREMENTS DOCUMENTS 
 
4.1 
NASA-STD-6001 
 
NASA-STD-6001 describes material flammability tests and requires a system flammability 
evaluation for materials that fail those tests. All flight hardware used in NASA crewed space 
programs are required to comply with the flammability testing requirements of NASA-STD-
6001 or be assessed to prohibit fire propagation based on the (presumed flammable) hardware 
design or configuration.  
 
Note: NASA-STD-6001 contains several required material flammability tests: 
 
 
Test 1 – Upward Flame Propagation

Test 4 – Electrical Wire Insulation Flammability  
 
Test 17 – Upward Flammability of Materials in GOX

It also contains several supplemental tests (e.g., NASA-STD-6001, section A.2.4, 
Configurational Flammability [Test 10]1), that may be used, as needed, for further evaluation of 
materials that fail the basic tests or for testing special hardware configurations.  
 
For general-purpose solid materials used in vehicle crew compartments, the fundamental 
flammability test is Test 1, or a special configurational variation of this test.  Test 1 can be 
performed on single materials or on layups/combinations of materials.  Test 4 is used for 
electrical wire insulation in power circuits. The other tests are not required for payloads or any 
other flight program. Test 17 applies only to pressurized oxygen systems. (Test 1 may also be 
used for oxygen systems at pressures below 50 pounds per square inch absolute [psia]). 
 
4.2 
NASA-STD-6016, Standard Materials and Processes Requirements for Spacecraft 
 
Hardware used in NASA programs, including the MPCV, SLS, Gateway, HLS, and CCP, is 
required to meet the intent of NASA-STD-6016. This Standard requires Materials Usage 
Agreements (MUAs) for hardware containing materials that do not meet the NASA-STD-6001 
flammability requirements. The guidelines in this handbook can be used for hardware 
flammability assessment to support MUA acceptance rationale.  
 
Note: Versions of NASA-STD-6016 released prior to Revision A contained an appendix with 
standard MUA rationale codes for the most common acceptable configurations containing 
flammable materials. In subsequent releases, these codes were replaced with rationale language 
in the flammability control section detailing specific approved exceptions to the flammability 
requirements in NASA-STD-6001. The NASA-STD-6016C flammability requirements are 
reproduced in Appendix A of this handbook. 
 
NASA-STD-6016 requires materials to be nonflammable or self-extinguishing in their use 
configuration as defined by NASA-STD-6001, Test 1, or an appropriate configurational 
flammability test per NASA-STD-6001, with several exceptions. The basis of several of these 
exceptions is explained in this handbook.  
 
This release of JSC 29353 refers explicitly to NASA-STD-6016C as the flammability 
requirements will be revised in future revisions.  The most significant NASA-STD-6016C 
exception that will be revised is for materials used in minor quantities with dimensions 
controlled so the potential fire propagation path is less than 15 linear centimeters (cm) (6 linear 
inches]) and the surface area is no more than 80 square centimeters (cm2) (12 square inches) in

crew environments2, and 30 linear cm (12 linear inches) for external materials that are exposed 
only to air during ground processing, launch, and landing with no identified propagation path or 
ignition source. The exception for pressurized environments was based on the NASA-STD-6001, 
Test 1 flammability acceptance criterion (no test specimen of five standard-sized specimens [30

materials must be sufficiently separated from other flammable materials so that fire cannot 
“jump” from one flammable material to another. “No propagation path” also means that, if such 
materials are ignited, they must be unable to ignite adjacent materials that pass the standard 
NASA-STD-6001 flammability test but can be ignited by a more severe ignition source than the 
standard igniter used in NASA-STD-6001 testing directly or by transfer of burning debris (this 
aspect has not been considered adequately in the past).  A 15-cm (6-inch) fire break separation is 
normally adequate. Smaller separations may be acceptable, but design-specific testing is required 
for verification.   
 
NASA-STD-6016 requires MUAs for hardware containing materials that do not meet the NASA-
STD-6001 flammability requirements. As such, document hardware configurations analyzed and 
accepted for flammability via the approach laid out in this handbook in an approved MUA.  
 
 
5. 
FLAMMABILITY ASSESSMENT GUIDELINES 
 
The following guidelines provide assessment procedures that allow users to evaluate 
flammability hazards associated with their equipment. Users can employ these guidelines as a 
similarity basis for certifying their hardware. For payloads, include an explanation of this 
assessment process and its results in the hazard report. 
 
Existing test data on material flammability characteristics can be obtained by consulting the 
MSFC Materials and Processes Technical Information System (MAPTIS) database, which can be 
accessed via the Request Form at http://maptis.nasa.gov. When using MAPTIS data, understand 
that materials flammability is affected by the material thickness, test conditions, and any 
substrate to which the material is attached. Fabric seams and added flammable materials such as 
hook-and-loop fasteners, zippers and sewing thread may affect the flammability of materials 
used in assembled hardware.  Detailed configurations within such hardware may need to be 
tested to verify that materials identified by MAPTIS as meeting flammability requirements still 
meet those requirements in their use configuration.  Coatings and adhesive tapes have commonly 
been tested on aluminum substrates and may be substantially more flammable on nonmetallic 
substrates or (in the case of tapes) when used to wrap nonmetallic materials.  Flammability data 
taken from materials on aluminum substrates has historically been applied to materials on other 
metal substrates of equal or greater thickness.  However, aluminum has a higher thermal 
conductivity than many metals so this approach may not be valid.

When a flammability assessment results in an unacceptable configuration, reduction of 
flammability hazards is necessary to correct the flammability problems. The primary methods 
used by NASA to reduce flammability hazards are the limitation of flammable materials by 
replacement with nonflammable materials and the restriction of propagation paths, either by 
covering flammable materials with a nonflammable material or by separation of flammable 
materials. 
 
When the results of the flammability configuration analysis are inconclusive, the hardware 
configuration (or a simulated configuration with acceptable fidelity) may be tested for 
flammability to determine acceptability. The responsible hardware organization should contact 
the appropriate NASA Materials and Processes (M&P) organization to determine what testing is 
required. Alternatively, the hardware organization may choose to assume the configuration is 
flammable and implement appropriate measures to eliminate the flammability hazard. 
 
To conduct a flammability configuration assessment, use the following procedures.  
 
5.1 
Define the Environment 
 
Material flammability depends strongly on the oxygen concentration in the environment to which 
the materials will be exposed. The effect of system pressure is weaker but still significant. Before 
starting any flammability assessment, it is necessary to define the maximum oxygen 
concentration and associated pressure in the use environment. The maximum oxygen 
concentration is estimated from the maximum oxygen concentration allowed by the 
environmental control system oxygen concentration control band plus the maximum oxygen 
concentration sensor error, so it will be appreciably higher than the nominal oxygen 
concentration. Table 1, Maximum Oxygen Concentrations and Pressures for Crewed Spacecraft, 
shows maximum oxygen concentrations and pressures for crewed spacecraft in current NASA 
programs. 
 
The gravity level for the use application may be significant. Limited data exist suggesting that 
flammability in partial gravity (lunar and Martian) may be worse than in Earth’s gravity or in 
microgravity without forced convection (see Ferkul and Olson, 2010; Sacksteder and T’ien, 
1994; Osorio et al., 2015). While more is known or can be determined about the effect of oxygen 
concentration, HLS and EHP missions to the Moon and Mars require that partial gravity impacts 
be accounted for to the extent possible. This handbook does not consider the effects of partial 
gravity because available data are currently too limited. As more data become available, account 
for the effect of lunar or Martian gravity to the greatest extent possible. 
 
The design solutions in this handbook for controlling materials flammability are appropriate for 
environments to 30 percent oxygen at 10.2 psia, unless otherwise noted. Past NASA programs 
used much higher oxygen concentrations (i.e., 100 percent for Apollo and 70 percent for Skylab). 
Per Human Exploration and Operations (HEO) Systems Engineering and Integration (SE&I) 
Decision Memorandum HEO-DM-1006 (2021), habitable exploration elements that operate on 
the lunar or Martian surface will use a minimum oxygen concentration of 33.7 percent at 8.2 
psia. The maximum oxygen concentration for the HLS Program is specified to be 37 percent, but

concentration/pressure combination. (The ISS airlock prebreathe environment always bounds the 
normal flammability ISS environment of 24.1 percent oxygen maximum at 14.7 psia.) 
 
5.2 
Evaluate the Overall Hardware Configuration 
 
Flight hardware is often in the form of a “black box,” a container with internal electronics, 
experiments, etc. Such containers are frequently effective at containing internal fires. Sealed 
containers have no vent openings and a verified maximum leak rate. Vented containers have 
active vents that facilitate the replenishment of oxygen during combustion. These containers may 
or may not contain associated cooling airflow. Intermediate containers have no active vents or 
cooling airflow but are not physically sealed to prevent air exchange; many commercial off-the-
shelf (COTS) electronics items fall into this intermediate category. 
 
1. If the hardware is a closed box without vents or power, then the materials inside the 
box will not contribute to the fire hazard unless the box is constructed from a non-effective fire 
barrier material―the box acts as a fire barrier. 
 
2. If the electrical power within the box is exclusively from alkaline batteries, no 
ignition can occur in Earth’s gravity or microgravity. NASA testing of solid materials in an 
atmosphere containing 34 percent oxygen at 15 psia previously resulted in a conclusion that they 
cannot be ignited by electrical powers of less than 25 watts (see WSTF # 07-42203, 2008)3.  
However, recent reevaluation of that testing found that the actual voltage in the test was 
considerably lower than reported and the power was more like 5 watts. Further testing is required 
before a new power threshold can be identified. Small, low-voltage alkaline and coin batteries 
are still believed to be incapable of delivering powers required for ignition, although larger 
batteries (such as lithium-ion rechargeable batteries for portable electronic devices) can certainly 
achieve higher power levels. 
 
Note: Combustion theory and very limited experimental data indicate that electrical power 
required for ignition may be lower in partial gravity environments than in Earth’s gravity. In the 
absence of specific data, use a maximum short-circuit power draw less than 2 watts for lunar 
and Martian environments.

5.3 
Evaluate the Way the Hardware Will Be Used 
 
Hardware that is normally stowed in a fireproof/fire barrier container and exposed to the cabin 
environment for short periods during use may be acceptably controlled by compliance with 
stowage constraints (see section 6 of this handbook). Fireproof/fire barrier containers provided 
by NASA include stowage lockers that fully enclose hardware and stowage bags such as the ISS 
and MPCV cargo transfer bags (CTB) which use a Nomex® fabric that meets flammability 
requirements in 30 percent oxygen at 10.2 psia (see section 8.6 of this handbook). 
 
The acceptability of the ISS and MPCV CTBs is currently uncertain.  Although the Nomex 
acreage has been verified to meet flammability requirements in up to 30% oxygen at 10.2 psia, 
the effects of small areas of added flammable items are more significant than previously realized.  
In recent large-scale flammability testing for the HLS Program, CTBs designed so the acreage 
met flammability requirements propagated fire, with burning seams, hook-and-loop fasteners, 
and/or plastic zippers providing enough energy to ignite the Nomex acreage.  
 
For general purpose hardware, passing the standard flammability test using the standard igniter 
adequately protects against ignition by primary ignition sources, nearly all of which are 
electrical, other than lithium-ion battery runaway failures.  However, hardware items like CTBs 
that are used as fire barriers to protect flammable items stored inside need to be more robust 
from a flammability standpoint and also protect from secondary ignition sources.  Secondary 
ignition sources are known flammable materials that have been ignited by a primary ignition 
source.  The HLS testing showed that materials that did not ignite when exposed to a standard 
igniter did ignite when exposed to burning socks ignited by a standard igniter.  NASA-STD-6016 
does not currently address this concern and the current NASA-STD-6001 configurational 
flammability test is not adequate to address it; but future revisions of these standards will address 
the issue. 
 
Note: Nonflammable Nomex® has always been considered to be an excellent fire barrier material 
because it does not melt and forms a char when exposed to a flame. It should still be effective for 
containments designed to address the above concerns, although configurational testing will be 
required, at least until guidance on acceptable configurations can be established.  Also, Nomex® 
flammability varies with brand, weight, and color. Flammability data for tested brands, weights, 
and colors of Nomex® are in MAPTIS; if data do not exist for the specific Nomex® being used, 
test the Nomex® to verify acceptability. 
 
Conduct a flammability analysis for hardware that is permanently mounted in a rack or a locker 
space.

5.4 
Identify the Major Materials to be Assessed 
 
a. Materials that are unexposed, overcoated with a fire-blocking material that has been 
verified for the use environment are acceptable and do not need to be assessed.  Materials that 
are no more than 0.25 inch thick and sandwiched between nonporous, nonflammable materials 
with only the edges exposed may be acceptable provided that the nonporous, nonflammable 
material doesn’t melt and expose the flammable material underneath; at this time, testing is 
required to verify acceptability.  Assess any other material within a crew-habitable compartment.  
As noted in section 4.2 of this handbook, materials must be sufficiently separated from other 
flammable materials so that fire cannot “jump” from one flammable material to another, with a 
15-cm (6-inch) fire break separation normally adequate and design-specific testing required to 
verify the acceptability of smaller separations. The flammable material must be unable to ignite 
adjacent materials that pass the NASA-STD-6001 flammability test but might be ignited by a 
higher energy ignition source.   
 
Flammable materials with a continuous linear dimension less than 4 inches or an area less than 4 
square inches are acceptable on metallic substrates provided that they do not release burning 
drips that could ignite other materials.  NASA-STD-6016C allows materials with any continuous 
linear dimension less than 6 inches or an area less than 12 square inches (and does not clearly 
limit such materials to metallic substrates) but this allowance will change for future revisions of 
NASA-STD-6016. 
 
b.  Assess any material outside of a crew-habitable compartment if it occurs in a 
quantity with any continuous area greater than 12 square inches. The boundary of the material to 
be assessed is the point where there is sufficient separation from other flammable materials to 
prevent fire propagation (see section 4.2 of this handbook).  
 
c. Metallic panels and structures, including titanium, are nonflammable when tested per 
NASA-STD-6001, Test 1, in environments containing 30 percent oxygen or less and need not be 
considered unless the environment exceeds this level4. Stainless steels and aluminum alloys are 
not flammable at higher oxygen concentrations such as those for habitable exploration elements 
on lunar or Martian surfaces (see Benning, Zabrenski, and Le, 1988; ASTM MNL36, 2007). This 
may also apply for magnesium and titanium, but data do not exist. Metallic screens may be 
flammable, even at ISS oxygen concentrations.  Verify metallic screens to be nonflammable if 
used alone or to be non-ignitable if used as a fire barrier material.  Note that as-purchased vent 
screen material may have a residual coating of flammable lubricant used in the screen 
manufacturing process. The lubricant needs to be removed for effective use as a fire barrier 
material. 
 
d. Ceramics are nonflammable in environments containing up to 40 percent oxygen at 
pressures up to 14.7 psia and need not be considered for such environments.

following key factors in material flammability when using MAPTIS flammability data:  
 
a. Oxygen concentration – If MAPTIS flammability data obtained at higher oxygen 
concentrations and the same pressure show that a material is acceptable, then it is generally 
acceptable at lower concentrations and the identical pressure. An A-rated material at 30 percent 
oxygen is almost always acceptable for use at lower oxygen concentrations.  Since even small 
increases in oxygen concentration can change the flammability rating, a material that is 
acceptable in 24.1 percent oxygen may be flammable in 24.5 percent oxygen at the same 
pressure.  
 
Note: Hardware designed for internal or external use on ISS is acceptable for transport in 
pressurized areas of vehicles with higher oxygen concentrations, provided it is unpowered and 
stowed in a locker or validated fire barrier stowage bag. Unpowered metal boxes, which may be 
painted, are also acceptable. Assess hardware not meeting these constraints for flammability at 
the higher oxygen concentration and pressure. 
 
b. Pressure – This parameter for the environments under consideration usually has a 
much smaller effect on flammability than oxygen concentration, but it should not be ignored. At 
a given oxygen concentration, higher pressures are generally more flammable than lower 
pressures. Relatively large changes in pressure are generally required to alter a material’s 
flammability rating. The effects are material-dependent and should be evaluated (see Hirsch, 
Williams, Haas, Beeson, Ruff, and Pedley, 2009).

5.5 
Determine the Fire Propagation Paths 
 
Determine whether externally exposed materials (including container housings) represent fire 
propagation paths exceeding 6 inches in crew-habitable compartments or 12 inches in other 
areas. For any given material application, propagation from one flammable material application 
to the next is not acceptable. Fire propagation paths can be limited by appropriately sized fire 
breaks. Verify effective fire break spacing via testing or via existing data by similarity at a given 
set of use conditions. If fire propagation is possible, then take positive action to control or 
eliminate the hazard. Sample solutions are included in sections 6 and 7 of this handbook.  
 
5.6 
Lithium-Ion and Other High Specific Energy Batteries 
 
High specific energy cells and batteries such as rechargeable lithium-ion and large lithium cells 
and batteries contain considerable stored energy and may be a credible ignition source under 
abuse or other off-nominal operating conditions. A far more serious concern is the potential for 
lithium-ion batteries to catch fire as a result of thermal runaway. Such fires are extremely rare 
(an evaluation of statistics for 2017 for lithium-ion batteries in portable consumer electronics 
devices showed a 1 in 8 million occurrence of a battery cell fire [see NASA JSC ISS Safety 
Review Panel Acrylic Cupola Scratch Pane Assembly Replacement TIM Minutes, 2018] but are 
high energy and capable of igniting materials that pass the NASA-STD-6001 flammability test.  
Faraday Insights Issue 17, July 2023, states that rates of catastrophic lithium-ion battery failure 
(fire or explosion) remain extremely low, ranging from 1 in 10 million to 1 in 40 million cells 
experiencing such a failure.

5.7 
Clothing 
 
The use of flammable outer clothing has long been accepted for the Space Shuttle and ISS 
Programs. With the exception of exercise clothing (see below), the only restriction has been that 
the flammable material forms a char when burning, rather than melting, which would result in 
severe burns. To meet this restriction, outer clothing has usually been limited to 100 percent 
cotton or verified nonflammable Nomex®. Wool, some cotton/polyester blends, and other 
flammable materials that have been shown to form a char have been accepted. Materials used for 
underwear have not been restricted for flammability because they are covered by outer clothing. 
Flammable socks have not been restricted as their mass is too small to create a propagation path 
concern. Synthetic exercise clothing has been permitted during the Space Shuttle and ISS 
Programs because use is limited to exercise periods. Any flammable clothing is required to be 
stowed in validated fire barrier containers when not worn. 
 
This approach on clothing flammability also applies to MPCV missions, which have the same 
worst-case environment of 30 percent oxygen at 10.2 psia as the Space Shuttle Program. This 
approach is not expected to be acceptable for HLS and other EVA-intensive exploration missions 
with maximum oxygen concentrations of 36-40 percent at 8.2 psia.  The Gateway Program will 
have the same worst-case environment as MPCV, but no decision has been made on the 
acceptability of flammable clothing. 
 
 
6. 
CONTAINERS 
 
Evaluate the fire containment capability of containers according to the amount of fuel involved, 
container wall characteristics, and the presence of a combustion-supporting environment. 
 
6.1 
Sealed Containers 
 
Hermetically sealed containers have a verified low leak rate and may be filled with an inert gas 
such as nitrogen. Environmentally sealed containers also have a verified low leak rate (higher

APPROVED FOR PUBLIC RELEASE — DISTRIBUTION IS UNLIMITED 
than hermetically sealed containers) and they normally contain air. Containers with a helium leak 
rate less than 1 × 10-4 cm3/second are considered to be sealed. 
 
Fire propagation in a sealed container depends upon the container structural configuration. If the 
sealed container contains an inert gas, then it can be assumed that fire will not be initiated. 
Further, it may be assumed that for sealed metal containers with an air environment, or a 
spacecraft atmosphere similar to those in Table 1 in this handbook, fire will be contained if the 
container wall is at least 0.060 inch thick.7 The same assumption would apply to nonmetallic 
containers, provided the container materials are nonflammable and are not melted by an internal 
fire. Even flammable nonmetallic containers may be able to contain internal fires in 
configuration if edges are not exposed and the fire does not breach the container exterior (see 
section 9.6 in this handbook). 
 
For the much higher oxygen concentrations during the Apollo and Skylab Programs, the 
container internal void space was a significant factor in flammability assessment and was 
generally limited to less than 30 percent of its total volume. For ISS and expected future crewed 
mission environments, a larger void space is acceptable; and the normal (tight) packing of flight 
electronics limits the internal void space acceptably, as demonstrated by the simulated MPCV 
avionics box testing in 40 percent oxygen described in section 9.6 of this handbook. 
 
6.2 
Vented Containers  
 
Materials inside vented, fire-proof/validated fire barrier containers will not propagate fire if vent 
sizes are limited and insufficient oxygen is present to support prolonged combustion. A fire may 
propagate if forced air convection is present and/or the container vent area is a large percentage 
of the total surface area, facilitating the replenishment of oxygen during combustion. Tests have 
shown that vented containers will contain fires if the container vents are covered with a fine 
metal screen (at least 60 mesh) using fire-resistant metals (e.g., stainless steel or nickel) rather 
than relatively flammable metals (e.g., titanium, or magnesium), or if the vent area is less than 1 
percent of the total surface area8. Although relatively flammable, 60-mesh aluminum is more 
effective as a vent screen than coarser aluminum screen material and more effective than 60-
mesh stainless steel because of its higher thermal conductivity. A vent screen may not prevent 
ignition of highly flammable materials located immediately outside the screen, so keep 
flammable materials away from container vents. For other vented container configurations, 
address conditions (such as initial oxygen concentration, airflow, vent type, and vent locations) 
that could lead to uncontrolled fires.

very high flow velocities, the airflow will prevent maintenance of stable flames, thus “blowing 
out” the fire. The intermediate flow velocities represent the worst case. The worst-case flow 
velocity is configuration dependent and may be affected by gravity and pressure conditions. It is 
difficult to determine by analysis the acceptable flow velocities for a specific vented container 
configuration. An exception is laptop computers and similar items that have slow forced airflow 
and tightly packed electronics; such items can be assumed to have negligible air flow. 
 
6.3 
Intermediate Containers 
 
As noted previously, intermediate containers are not airtight but do not have active vents or 
airflow. They are a common configuration in crewed NASA spacecraft. Examples include 
avionics boxes that are cold-plate cooled, not sealed, and may have covered vents for pressure 
equalization; fully enclosed NASA-provided stowage lockers and nonflammable stowage bags 
(note the issues with current CTB designs identified in Section 5.3); and most commercial 
electronics items that do not contain an internal cooling fan. Note that the MPCV stowage 
lockers are not full enclosed as the inner walls are in grid form, not solid and the Nomex fabric 
ISS Zero-G Softracks may have similar issues to the CTBs. 
 
NASA-provided fully enclosed stowage lockers and validated fire barrier stowage bags can be 
treated as containers that act as barriers to external fire. Flammable materials stowed in these 
containers do not constitute a fire risk while in the containers, provided they are unpowered. 
Evaluate powered payloads/experiments stowed in lockers for an entire mission or located in 
place of a locker from a flammability standpoint because they present a potential fire hazard.  
For atmospheres containing up to 30% oxygen at 10.2 psia, many commercial electronics items 
can be addressed through the stowage constraints described in section 7 of this handbook. Items 
that do not comply with these stowage constraints may be acceptable if the case can be shown to 
be nonflammable and capable of containing an internal fire. Many commercial electronic items 
can be obtained in metallic or nonflammable polycarbonate cases and the internal components 
have a high packing density that minimizes the internal void space.  
 
In addition, many small commercial items are powered internally by alkaline button, or coin cell 
batteries. Such batteries are not considered to be capable of delivering sufficient energy to ignite

7. 
STOWED HARDWARE 
 
Many small, commercial, off-the-shelf components are used in a spacecraft. Examples include 
cameras, power tools, compact and digital video and audio systems, and tablet computers; 
medical devices and medications; clothing; and personal hygiene items. Most of these items are 
stowed in lockers or validated fire barrier stowage bags at use conditions, taken out only as 
required, and returned after use. The majority of this hardware for ISS and MPCV is provided as 
NASA Johnson Space Center (JSC) government-furnished equipment or by the ISS Cargo 
Mission Contract. Similar arrangements have been initiated for Gateway and HLS. JSC has 
determined that such stowed hardware is acceptable for flammability in atmospheres to 30 
percent oxygen at 10.2 psia regardless of the materials used, provided the component has limited 
use10, is crew tended when in use, and is stowed in validated fire barrier stowage containers or 
lockers when not in use. 
 
Setting specific flight rules for each hardware item controlled by stowage is considered to be an 
unreasonable imposition on crew operations. During the Space Shuttle Program, NASA relied on 
crew housekeeping, which was sufficient to ensure that flammable items were returned to 
stowage after use. The same approach was not effective for ISS, because the much larger 
pressurized volume allowed for many unstowed items to be left out indefinitely (commonly 
attached to walls with hook-and-loop fasteners). In response, ISS developed a generic 
Operational Control Agreement Database (OCAD) for control of flammable items on ISS. At the 
time of writing, this OCAD is currently OCAD 122607. ISS OCAD numbers do change when 
any changes are made to the content.

higher oxygen concentration environments.

8. 
FLAMMABILITY REDUCTION METHODS 
 
This section describes common methods used to control flammability hazards. These methods 
include replacement of flammable materials with nonflammable alternatives and methods of 
protecting flammable materials by covering with validated fire barrier materials. These 
flammability reduction methods may not be effective for the high oxygen concentrations 
required for EVA-intensive exploration missions. 
 
8.1  
General Materials Protection 
 
Commercial items with flammable outer surfaces (such as acrylonitrile butadiene styrene [ABS], 
polyvinyl chloride [PVC], polyethylene, and/or polyamides [nylons]) may be covered with a 
validated fire barrier tape. A 0.003-inch-thick aluminum tape protects most plastics, foam, and 
cardboard from external flame initiation, including protection in 40 percent oxygen at 10.2 psia.  
(See WSTF 22-48300.) Acrylic adhesives are, themselves, extremely flammable; and narrow 
tapes with acrylic adhesives and significant overlap may not provide adequate protection. Tapes 
with silicone adhesives perform notably better. Even Federal Specification L-T-80, Tape, 
Pressure Sensitive Adhesive (Aluminum-Backed), aluminum tape with an acrylic adhesive may 
be ineffective when used to wrap items like wire harnesses. If aluminum tape cannot be used for 
electrical reasons, then Kapton® polyimide tape with a silicone adhesive will provide the same 
protection for oxygen concentrations to 30 percent at 10.2 psia.  
 
Other nonflammable tapes such as nonflammable fiberglass tape with a silicone adhesive11 may 
also work, but testing is required to verify that the nonflammable tape is validated as a fire 
barrier and is able to protect the shielded flammable material from burning. Flammable PVC-
insulated electrical cables have been shown to burn in 30 percent oxygen at 10.2 psia when 
covered by nonflammable Fluorinated Ethylene Propylene (FEP) Teflon® tape, nonflammable 
Nitto P-212 glass cloth tape, or nonflammable 3M 361 glass cloth tape. (See WSTF 18-47448, 
WSTF #20-47957, and WSTF #20-47967.) The FEP Teflon® tape does not provide adequate

For applications where tape is aesthetically unacceptable but fire protection is needed, the 
flammable surfaces may be coated with a validated fire barrier material such as a 
fluoroelastomer. NASA has used fluoroelastomer coatings successfully in environments up to 30 
percent oxygen at 10.2 psia. The coating application can be complex, and the coating is easily 
damaged. The original coating selected was Fluorel® (hexafluoropropene and vinylidene fluoride 
copolymer) mill stock, but this material is no longer available. Other fluoroelastomer mill stock 
materials have been used. These fluoroelastomer coatings have not been tested for higher oxygen 
concentrations and are not expected to be effective for habitable exploration elements on the 
lunar or Martian surface12. Verify the effectiveness of a nonflammable fire barrier coating by test 
because coating and/or substrate melting can lead to the substrate burning. 
 
The most common nonmetallic case materials for commercial items used in space flight (such as 
cameras, camcorders, video and audio players, and laptop computers) are ABS and 
polycarbonates. ABS is flammable and polycarbonates are normally acceptable at oxygen 
concentrations to 30 percent. Polycarbonate/ABS blends are acceptable for use in ISS 
environments up to 24.1 percent oxygen at 14.7 psia. In many cases, the responsible hardware 
organization may be able to select a commercial item with a metallic or polycarbonate case, thus 
eliminating the need for wrapping or coating with a validated fire barrier material.  When 
commercial hardware with a polycarbonate case will be used at oxygen concentrations above 
24.1%, conduct testing to verify the acceptability of the polycarbonate case. 
 
Electrically powered items with internal flammable materials can usually be treated as fire-
resistant containers for internal fires. In some cases, even a highly flammable case can be an 
adequate fire barrier against propagation of an internal fire to the outside of the container. 
(Section 9.6 of this handbook shows an example.) An extreme option is to fill the component 
internal volume with a nonflammable material (such as a nonflammable, glass-filled, epoxy-
potting compound) to provide acceptable flammability protection from internal ignition sources.  
 
8.2 
Wire and Cable 
 
Most aerospace-grade electrical wire insulation is nonflammable in crewed environments. 
Limitations are usually driven by other factors such as flexibility and cut-through resistance. 
Below are some examples of nonflammable insulation: 
 
1. Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) (SAE AS22759, Wire, electric, fluoropolymer-
insulated, copper or copper alloy, formerly MIL-W-22759; ANSI/NEMA-WC-27500, Standard 
for Aerospace and Industrial Electrical Cable, formerly MIL-C-27500); or equivalent) - good 
general-purpose wire insulation that has high flexibility but poor cut-through resistance.

specification SSQ 21652, Wire, Electric, Silicone Insulated, Nickel Coated Copper, Space

Electrical wiring found in commercial off-the-shelf hardware typically has PVC, polyethylene, 
or chloroprene insulation. These insulation materials are flammable in ISS and exploration 
enriched-oxygen environments and their use is discouraged. (PVC-insulated wiring is usually 
nonflammable in air.) They may be used when demonstrated to be acceptable in a design-
specific configuration by a flammability configuration analysis or test. Commercial wiring inside 
electronics boxes and signal wiring outside such boxes can usually be accepted by this method. 
External power cables nearly always need to be replaced or protected from ignition. Flammable 
insulation is acceptable on wires in external payloads that are not powered after receipt by 
NASA until the payload is installed in a vacuum13.

concentrations planned for habitable exploration elements on the lunar or Martian surface.

materials. Lacing cords made from PTFE, PTFE/fiberglass, or verified nonflammable Nomex® 
and cable ties made from ETFE or ethylene chlorotrifluoroethylene (ECTFE) fluoropolymers are 
acceptable for oxygen concentrations to 30 percent oxygen at 10.2 psia but may be flammable at 
higher oxygen concentrations. Flammable cable tie wraps may be used on nonflammable cables,

8.3 
Electrical Connectors 
 
In air and moderately enriched-oxygen environments (up to 40 percent oxygen), the shell of a 
metal shell connector prevents fire propagation from the nonmetallic materials used inside the 
connector to other nonmetallic materials, regardless of the material inside the connector. The 
configuration is always acceptable for flammability, and testing is not required. 
 
Testing or flammability configuration analysis is required for nonmetallic shell connectors. The 
acceptability of flammable polymeric materials used internal to the connector depends on the 
flammability of the shell material and its ability to act as a fire barrier. 
 
8.4 
Tubes and Hoses 
 
External tubes or hoses such as a vacuum cleaner hose made from flammable materials may be 
replaced with a nonflammable material or covered with a validated fire barrier material. Clear 
PTFE or FEP Teflon® tubes and hoses are readily available to replace flammable materials. If 
flammable tubes or hoses are used, the exterior can be protected by a validated fire barrier fabric 
covering such as nonflammable Nomex®, PBI, and Beta cloth15. If a polymeric fire barrier 
material is used, verify its effectiveness by test; if the sleeve is sewn together, verify that the 
seam will survive exposure to a fire.  Also assess the potential for internal ignition of the tube 
walls. 
 
Tubing and hoses used in medical experiments are usually flammable and cannot be replaced or 
covered without compromising the experiment. Such hardware can usually be accepted as 
complying with the stowage guidelines in section 6 of this handbook.

8.7 
Thermal Control Blankets 
 
Thermal control blankets are the most widely used potentially flammable external materials. 
These blankets typically contain 12 to 40 layers of 0.0005- to 0.002-inch-thick film separated by 
scrim cloth. Blanket materials are usually constructed of metal-coated polyethylene terephthalate 
or polyimide film with an organic separator scrim. For durability, the inner and outer layers are 
generally thicker than the internal layers. The outer layer has controlled optical properties and is 
usually polyimide, silver Teflon®, or Beta cloth. 
 
Acceptable thermal control blankets are typically constructed as follows:  
 
1. The outer layer is made of a validated fire barrier material such as polyimide film (at 
least 0.0015 inch thick), silver Teflon®, or Beta cloth.  
 
2. Internal layers can be a combination of flammable films or scrims.  
 
3. Edges are hemmed or suitably finished so that the inner flammable layers are 
protected.  
 
For exposure to spacecraft external environments, assess erosion of outer surfaces by low-Earth-
orbit atomic oxygen and embrittlement by ultraviolet radiation. 
 
8.8 
Composites and Fiber-Related Laminates 
 
Graphite-epoxy composite structures are generally nonflammable in the ISS environment of 24.1 
percent oxygen at 14.7 psia. Composite flammability is dependent on the specific construction, 
thickness configuration, and resins used, with a few being flammable in 24.1 percent oxygen at 
14.7 psia and at least one being nonflammable in 100 percent oxygen at 10.2 psia (Hirsch, 
Williams, Harper, Beeson, Pedley, 2007). Bismaleimide-based carbon composites have not been 
widely tested, but the bismaleimide resin is similar to polyimides, so they are likely to be

TEST EXAMPLES 
 
The following test examples show the implementation of many of the flammability reduction 
methods previously discussed and their effectiveness as has been demonstrated by test. These 
examples, together with the guidelines in earlier sections, can be used to assess the fire risk of 
other configurations. Many hardware configurations can be assessed by similarity to these design 
solutions or compliance to the guidelines without the need for testing. Unless specified 
otherwise, all tests used a chemical igniter complying with the ISO 14624-1 specifications, as 
required by NASA-STD-6001. 
 
9.1 
Materials Selection 
 
The ISS Program initially selected a portable Bluetooth speaker system with an ABS case. Since 
this speaker system is expected to be left out in the ISS vehicle for long periods, flammability 
cannot be adequately controlled through stowage. The Bluetooth® speaker burned completely 
when tested for flammability in 24.1 percent oxygen at 14.7 psia (see WSTF #14-46333). A 
comparable Bluetooth® speaker from another manufacturer has an aluminum case. It was 
undamaged and still operated after flammability testing in similar conditions (WSTF #14-
46338). Figure 1, Bluetooth® Speakers, shows the Bluetooth® speaker with the ABS case before 
and after test, and the Bluetooth speaker with the aluminum case after test.  
 
Note: These results should not be interpreted as suggesting either Bluetooth® speaker is 
superior for general purpose use on the ground. Flammability is not a discriminator when 
selecting such items for ground use―only in the specialized spacecraft environment.

tape was not identified.  As noted in Section 8.1, aluminum tape with a silicone adhesive is 
preferred, but aluminum tape with an acrylic adhesive should be effective for this kind of 
application where the tape is cleanly adhered to the flammable substrate.

9.4 
Sealed Containers 
 
Inert gas-filled sealed containers 
 
A limited number of tests have been conducted on internal ignition of inert gas-filled sealed 
containers (see NSTS 22648). The ignition source was internal electrical wiring, electrically 
overloaded until the wire insulation fused. The standard igniter was not used because it does not 
burn in the absence of oxygen. As expected, even when highly flammable materials were present 
inside the container, no ignition occurred; and the only damage was from the electrical overload.  
 
 
Oxygen-enriched gas-filled sealed containers 
 
Several tests have been conducted on sealed containers filled with enriched-oxygen 
environments. An example is the Apollo Program master events sequence controller. This item 
was tested with an internal atmosphere of 100 percent oxygen at 16 psia using a standard igniter 
(see NSTS 22648). Although all internal polymeric materials were flammable in this atmosphere, 
the resultant fire was contained with minor internal damage from local surface burning of the 
room-temperature vulcanizing silicone rubber coating on the circuitry and components (see 
Figure 6, Apollo Master Events Sequence Controller). The container internal void space for this 
test was approximately 30 percent. This test environment is a more demanding flammability 
environment than those identified for ISS and human exploration of space. 
 
9.5 
Vented Containers 
 
The Space Shuttle orbiter inertial measurement unit was a formed aluminum box containing 
polyurethane-coated circuit boards, chloroprene vent hoses, MIL-W-8104417 polyalkene-
insulated wire, and about 20 percent void space. The polyurethane coating, chloroprene hoses,  
and polyalkene-insulated wire were flammable. The inertial measurement unit was tested for 
internal flammability in 25.9 percent oxygen at 14.3 psia, and in 30 percent oxygen at 10.0 psia 
at its normal cooling flow rate of 6.3 standard cubic feet per minute (scfm) (see NSTS 22648). 
The unit passed both flammability tests with minimal damage. Figure 7, Space Shuttle Inertial 
Measurement Unit, shows the pretest and post-test conditions of this unit. 
 
A Space Shuttle orbiter proximity switch box constructed of sheet metal was also flammability 
tested (see NSTS 22648). This box contained electrical components, polyurethane-coated circuit 
boards, and 50 percent void space. Flammability tests were conducted with a gas flow rate of 1.1 
scfm in 25.9 percent oxygen at 14.3 psia, and in 30 percent oxygen at 10.0 psia. This unit also 
passed both flammability tests with minimal damage. Figure 8, Space Shuttle Orbiter Proximity 
Switch Box, shows the pretest and post-test conditions of this unit.

9.7 
Special Cases 
 
When a hardware design does not fit any of the previous examples, it may be possible to accept 
the hardware through a design-specific test program. An example is the testing that was 
conducted for the ISS commercial off-the-shelf vacuum cleaner power cable, intended to plug 
into the ISS power inverter. The vacuum cleaner (see Figure 17a, ISS COTS vacuum cleaner) is 
constructed of flammable materials but considered acceptable because it has no credible internal 
ignition sources, is always tended by the crew while in use, and is stowed in a fireproof/validated 
fire barrier container when not in use. The power cable is a standard household vacuum cleaner 
cable with PVC wire insulation wrapped in cotton and contained in a PVC jacket. An 
intentionally damaged cable is shown in Figure 17b, Damaged Section of vacuum cleaner cable.  
Although this construction is designed to be nonflammable in air, it is flammable in the ISS 
oxygen/pressure environment. Since a high current passes through the cable with serious 
potential to act as an ignition source, this cable was considered to be a significant potential fire 
risk. 
 
Because of this concern, a test program was conducted to determine whether ignition could occur 
as a result of an electrical short between damaged vacuum cleaner cable wires. The testing 
showed that such ignition could occur (see Figure 17c, Burned cable post-test), but only with 
cable damage so severe that it would have been visually detectable by crew members before it 
reached the point where it could cause ignition (see WSTF 12-45702). The configuration in 
Figure 17b, which resulted in ignition, had significant lengths of bare conductors with frayed 
cotton looped into intimate contact with the conductors at the electrical short location.  
 
Replacing the cable with a nonflammable cable would have impacted the intended ISS approach 
of minimizing hardware certification costs by purchasing commercial off-the-shelf items and 
using them powered by the ISS power inverter without modification. This cable would have been 
unacceptable if it had been planned to be deployed unattended for long periods because of the 
potential for ignition by possible external ignition sources. The stowage approach that was 
planned to control the vacuum cleaner flammability was adequate for the cable.

APPENDIX A: NASA-STD-6016C FLAMMABILITY 
REQUIREMENTS AND EXEMPTIONS 
 
A.1 
PURPOSE 
 
This Appendix provides the materials flammability control requirements of NASA-STD-6016C 
(2021) for information only. This content may not be the same as in the current release. 
Contractual program requirements will be as specified in the NASA-STD-6016 release, and 
NASA-STD-5027 release, if applicable. 
 
 
4.2.1.1  Flammability Control 
 
[MPR 44] Materials that are nonflammable or self-extinguishing in their use configuration as 
defined by NASA-STD-6001B, Test 1 or an appropriate configurational flammability test per 
NASA-STD-6001B, shall be used for flammability control with the following exceptions:  
 
a. Ceramics, metal oxides, and inorganic glasses are exempt.  
 
b. Materials that are designed to ignite and burn in their use application (e.g., 
pyrotechnic materials) are exempted from flammability requirements, provided that 
systems/experiments using such materials are designed so that the burning materials cannot 
act as an ignition source for other hardware.  
 
c. Materials used in minor quantities (i.e., dimensions controlled so the potential fire 
propagation path is less than 15 linear cm (6 linear inch) and the surface area is no more than 
80 cm2 (12 in2)) in crew environments and 30 linear cm (12 linear inch) for external 
materials) with no propagation path or ignition source.  
 
d. Materials used in sealed containers are exempt because insufficient oxygen is 
available to maintain combustion.  
 
e. Materials within vented electronics packages with metallic cases and no forced air 
convection are exempt because of the self-extinguishing effect of expanding combustion 
gases in a constrained volume.  
 
f. Materials that have been shown by test to meet the requirements of NASA-STD-
6001B may be used in an environment with an oxygen concentration lower than the test level 
without further testing, provided that the oxygen partial pressure is no greater than the partial 
pressure at the test level or general test data exist to demonstrate that the higher oxygen 
partial pressure is outweighed by the lower percentage concentration for the environments in 
question.  
 
g. Materials are acceptable when used on a metal substrate that provides an adequate 
heat sink. A heat sink is considered adequate in the use configuration by test or analysis. 
When testing is conducted, materials passing the flammability test on a metal substrate are
